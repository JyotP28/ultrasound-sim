Open on a Desktop or Laptop

This simulation requires a desktop or laptop computer as the main workstation. Your smartphone is used as the ultrasound probe — the physical device you hold and move in space.

1 Open SONO-ACADEMY on a desktop or laptop browser.
2 A 4-digit Room PIN will appear prominently in the top-right corner of the workstation.
3 Scan the QR code shown in the workstation or navigate to probe.html on this phone and enter the PIN.
4 Hold your phone like a transducer and begin scanning.

The Spark

After my faculty mentor, Dr. Julie Cary, shared a paper on ultrasound simulation with me (SHAPES, McGaffey et al., JVME 2026), I started wondering why opportunities to practice healthcare skills at home are still so limited. High-end clinical ultrasound units and commercial mannequin phantoms cost tens of thousands of dollars, naturally restricting hands-on transducer practice to scheduled, high-stakes laboratories.

Everyday Hardware as a Clinical Transducer

Our smartphones have accelerometers, gyroscopes, and increasingly powerful software. Could we use them to make ultrasound practice more accessible, letting students build familiarity with probe movements outside the classroom?

I started building a simulator to explore that idea. It is far from finished, but I wanted to see whether something I felt should exist could actually be built. By linking a mobile device over low-latency WebRTC data channels to a desktop GPU raymarching shader, the phone acts directly as a spatial transducer probe.

Embracing AI in Medical Education

I have very little formal computer science experience, and AI has made experimenting with ideas like this far more approachable than I expected. Rather than fearing AI in healthcare education, I believe we should actively embrace it to enhance the student experience by building intuitive, student-centered learning tools that democratize simulation.

A Proof of Concept

I think healthcare education has a lot of room to catch up with what is possible in simulation. I am sharing this as a proof of concept, not a finished product, but hopefully it encourages others to experiment with ideas of their own.

"Sometimes the first step toward a tool that should exist is simply trying to build it."
